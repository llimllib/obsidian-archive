> Everything is free now, that's what they say

From the very beginning of me using computers, there were two things that interested me about it: the math, and making software that people liked for one reason or another.

I found the coding _fun_ in and of itself, but it was rarely the point for me; I wanted to make computers do things for me, or draw pretty pictures, or solve a problem for somebody else.

Early in my career, I wanted to draw some binary trees in my used-book-store employer's inventory app, and I discovered that it was an interesting problem. So I dove into the literature, studied it, somehow got connected to the editors of Python magazine, and [wrote an article](https://llimllib.github.io/pymag-trees/) about how to draw nice-looking binary trees.

That's one of the things I made that caught on reasonably well; it's been cited a bunch of places and used in a bunch of others where it wasn't cited. I never made a cent off it (the editor told me to send him an invoice, and I literally didn't know what one was or how to do that) and I never cared to. The sharing of the object was the point of the thing - people reading it and maybe understanding a bit more, or being able to do something they otherwise wouldn't have been able to.

The skill to produce something like that, which took me an _enormous_ amount of time; is no longer socially or commercially valuable. You can ask an LLM, which has ingested my article, all the articles it references, and a hundred others besides, to produce it and 5 minutes later you'll have it.

> Everything I ever done, they're gonna give it away

Oddly, I'm mostly not upset about that! What I wanted was to be able to produce more knowledge and more things, and for other people to produce more things. What I wanted then, and what I want now, is for us to share with each other the incredible bounty of knowledge we possess.

And now, with LLMs, we can produce things in a few minutes that would have taken weeks just a few years ago. It's incredible! And so incredible that it's become boring and even tedious.

I was inspired to write this when [Aman Bhargava](https://bsky.app/profile/aman.bh/post/3n2o5bngja22t) poked fun at people creating AI map artifacts, and [Bill Morris](https://billmorris.io/shoals/2026/11/19/overexposure.html) wrote about his feelings as a result. I have conflicted and conflicting feelings about it, because I both [produce things](https://billmill.org/nba/) that I use AI to generate and feel annoyed and tired at the quantity of things produced and shared by others online.

It's no longer novel, and it no longer is impressive. But oftentimes the things are still cool, which is weird and I don't know how to feel about either the thing or its author.

> We're gonna do it anyway, even if it doesn't pay

For those of us that used to be distinguished by our willingness to make things for free, what's left? Words are free, software is free; both in the sense of being unchained and in the sense of being as valuable as something that is given away.

Our reach has suddenly been expanded massively, but our grasp has not yet expanded to fill the new space. One path forward is to explore this: to reach much further than you or others have done before.

Are there questions you can answer now that you could not before, with real effort and understanding? Are there systems you could build that are more flexible than before, or more solid, or more distributed, or more centralized? How can you take this new power, and rather than use it like we did yesterday, bend it forward towards tomorrow?

That sounds hopelessly millenially optimistic to me, but it really does feel to me like it's the most appropriate response I can make towards the technological space I find myself in.

> Someone hit the big score, they figured it out

The other path I see for people who want to create things is to break the chains of the communications structures that pen us in. Build software that creates real community, local community, deeper remote community. Human connection and progress must remain the ultimate goal of those who would make computing a positive force in the world.

Part of the reason that Facebook became Facebook is that creating Facebook was Very Hard. It's still hard, I'm sure!

But parts of that problem are no longer hard; what are those parts? How can we build software that creates less toxic relationships with computing than the Facebook machine? 

Building community is still hard work, and work with deep value. If we leave it to the toxic people and systems, we will end up with a more toxic community than we already have.

> Never minded working hard, it's who I'm working for

A lot of my personal identity and value has been bundled up for a long time with my own ability to generate software artifacts that people liked, and value is on uncertain to bad footing. I'm trying to figure out what exactly it is I do, here, where "here" is in this society of humans.

I don't have any big conclusion to this post, just a few swirling feelings related to change of identity.
